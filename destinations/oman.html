Getting around

Renting a car is the easiest way to see Oman's wadis, forts and desert, since public transport is limited. Mwasalat buses connect Muscat with Nizwa, Sur and Salalah, and taxis in Muscat are best booked through a local app or agreed in advance. A 4x4 is only needed for sand and steep mountain roads.

Where to sleep

Wild camping is widely accepted on beaches and in the desert if you're discreet and clean up, and it's the cheapest way to stay. Otherwise expect simple guesthouses and budget hotels, plus desert camps in the Wahiba Sands.

What to eat

  • Shuwa: marinated lamb slow-cooked underground for a day or more
  • Omani halwa: sticky sweet flavored with rosewater and saffron
  • Kahwa: cardamom-spiced coffee served with dates
  • Mashuai: whole grilled kingfish with lemon rice

Money

Cards are widely accepted in Muscat and larger towns, but carry cash rials for villages, fuel stations in remote areas and small cafes. Tipping isn't expected but is appreciated, and haggling is normal in souks.

Phone data

Prepaid SIMs from Omantel or Ooredoo are easy to buy at the airport with your passport, with good coverage in towns and patchy signal in mountains and desert.

Heads up

Wadis can flood fast after rain in the mountains, so check the forecast before hiking or swimming.

Oman travel: quick answers

How much does a day in Oman cost on a budget?

About $56 a day for one person, from $40 in Wadi Shab to $97 in Daymaniyat Islands. That covers a hostel dorm or simple guesthouse, local food, local transport and the usual entry fees, so a week runs about $392 before flights.

When is the best time to visit Oman?

November to March is the best time, with the most reliable weather for getting around.
